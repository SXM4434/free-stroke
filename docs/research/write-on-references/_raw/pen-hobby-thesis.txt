WHAT: John D. Hobby's "Rasterizing Curves of Constant Width" - fast linear-time algorithm for rendering curved lines with constant width stroke, foundational for METAFONT pen model

## Paper Overview

This paper by John D. Hobby (published in the Journal of the ACM, Volume 36, Issue 2, 1989) presents a fast, linear-time algorithm for generating high-quality pixel representations of curved lines.

## Core Algorithm

The method approximates selecting a circle whose diameter is the desired line width and turning on all pixels covered by the circle as it moves along the desired curve. However, the circle is replaced by a carefully chosen **polygon** whose deviations from the circle represent **subpixel corrections** designed to improve the aesthetic qualities of the rasterized curve.

## Key Innovation

Instead of using perfect circular envelopes (computationally expensive), Hobby's method:
1. Uses a polygon approximation of the circle
2. Applies subpixel adjustments at polygon corners
3. Achieves high-quality results with linear time complexity

## Applicability to METAFONT

Knuth adopted Hobby's envelope rasterization method in METAFONT to compute the envelope of an ellipse stroking along a Bézier curve. This approach:
- Computes the envelope in terms of the raster (pixels) rather than scalable curves
- Reduces computational geometry to manipulating line segments and polygons
- Avoids direct polynomial curve mathematics

This method is documented in **The METAFONTbook §524** where Knuth sidesteps the complex mathematics of computing smooth envelope contours by discretizing to the pixel grid.

## Significance for Stroke Rendering

The paper established that:
- Curved strokes can be rasterized efficiently without computing exact envelopes
- Subpixel polygon corrections improve quality over naive raster approximation
- Linear-time algorithms are feasible for quality stroke rendering

## Related Work

This technique influenced:
- Rasterization approaches in digital typesetting
- Pen model implementations in METAFONT
- Modern understanding of constant-width stroke rendering
