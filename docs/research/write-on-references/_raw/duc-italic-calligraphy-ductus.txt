# Italic Calligraphy Ductus: Entry/Exit Strokes

Paul Shaw's detailed ductus for the Italic hand, showing stroke sequences and directions.

## Row 1: Letters with Entry & Exit Strokes (l, i, d, p)

For **l** and **d**:
"Place the broad-edged nib under the ascender line" then "slide the pen slightly upward and diagonally" before curving at both top and bottom into hairlines.

## Row 2: Letters Without Entry or Exit

The **t** lacks an entry stroke.

**Closed forms** like **a**, **q**, and **b** have restricted entry/exit capabilities.

For **j** and **y**: Shaw warns: "Do not over-exaggerate this entry stroke" as it creates "a big sloppy curve."

## Row 3: Multi-stroke Letters (n, m, h, u, k)

These letters begin with entry strokes like the **i** group, follow standard construction, then conclude with exit strokes.

The instruction emphasizes treating curls "as the equivalent of an elbow: sharp but not sharp like a knife."

## Diagonal Strokes (Rows 4 & 5)

**Diagonal letters** should have entry strokes that are "knife-like" rather than sweeping curves.

## Special Cases

**The z**: Requires gentle sliding movements: "Do not overdo the wiggle or the z will look like a fluttering flag."

## General Principles

In calligraphy, **exemplars are fully written alphabets that include the ductus**, which indicates the order of each stroke needed to form the letter with directional arrows.

**Ductus as Suggestion**: "The more complicated a letter becomes the more ways there are to make it so a ductus is only a suggestion not a hard-and-fast mandate."

## Italic Hand Characteristics

- Practiced with a broad-edged tool (nib + ink, fountain pen, or marker)
- Written at a slant between 5 and 10 degrees
- Letters usually taller than wide
- Minuscules typically sized at 5 nib widths
